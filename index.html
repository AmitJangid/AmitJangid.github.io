Research Summaries

Postdoctoral Research

Evolution of small genetic regulatory networks & CNSR system

We study how biological networks evolve and expand in the context of the plant collaborative-non-self recognition self-incompatibility system. Self-incompatibility evolved to avoid self-fertilization among hermaphroditic plants. We constructed a novel theoretical framework that crucially affords interaction promiscuity and multiple distinct partners per protein. We demonstrated spontaneous self-organization of the population into distinct 'classes' with full between-class compatibility and a dynamic long-term balance between class emergence and decay.

Reconciling conflicting selection pressures

Complex biological systems should often reconcile conflicting selection pressures. We studied how such pressures shape the evolution of the self-incompatibility system in plants. We found that selection exerts asymmetric responses of amino acid frequencies, affecting female proteins considerably, but hardly the male. Using large deviations theory, we well-approximated the simulated frequencies and found agreement with genomic data.

A phase transition: self-compatibility vs self-incompatibility

Despite the ubiquity of mixed-mating, its evolutionary sources and dynamic stability remain elusive. Using population-level stochastic simulations, we modeled the effects of promiscuity of molecular recognition and inbreeding depression. We revealed a phase diagram with three regimes: complete self-incompatibility (SI), complete self-compatibility (SC), and a mixture combining both. This mixed mode, described here for the first time, exhibits vigorous, non-decaying fluctuations.

PhD Research

Modeling IFN-y and IL-4 mediated networks in Atopic Dermatitis

We modeled the networks of Th1, Th2, dendritic, and keratinocyte cells mediated by key cytokines (IFN-y and IL-4) to study Atopic Dermatitis. From sensitivity analysis, we observed that transitions between acute and chronic states may occur through smooth or abrupt paths. Depending on the nature of the cytokines, there is a possibility to drive the system from a bi-stable phase to acute by giving external stimulus.

Stress driven minimal p53 regulatory pathway

We constructed a stress-driven minimal model of the p53 regulatory network. Depending on the nature of the external stress, four distinct dynamical states of p53 were observed: active, apoptosis, pre-malignant, and cancer states. We found that transitions from cancer to any other state are irreversible if stress is either oscillatory or constant.

Nucleus and noise regulating cell fate decision

We built a population-based stochastic cellular model starting from a single stem cell. The model consists of nucleus position, gene-regulatory network, and stochastic segregation of transcription factors. The model shows that the nucleus's position towards the basal leads to more asymmetric division, while introducing noise in the nucleus position leads to more differentiated cells.
